# STEP 6 — Assumptions & Validation

## 1. Purpose

Peter Park Here is based on assumptions about customers, parking owners, pricing, marketplace behavior, and the operating model.

An assumption is a belief that has not yet been sufficiently validated.

For example:

> "People will be willing to rent unused parking spaces."

This is an **assumption**, not a fact.

The purpose of this step is to identify the assumptions that could cause the product or business model to fail and determine which ones deserve the most attention.

---

## 2. Key Assumption Areas

The initial assumptions fall into the following areas:

- Customer demand
- Owner willingness
- Pricing
- Availability
- Trust
- Safety
- Convenience
- Marketplace liquidity
- Payment
- Cancellation
- Location density

Not every assumption has equal importance or uncertainty.

---

## 3. Prioritizing Assumptions

We prioritize assumptions using two dimensions:

### Impact

How seriously would the product or business be affected if the assumption were wrong?

### Uncertainty

How little evidence do we currently have supporting the assumption?

The highest-priority assumptions are therefore:

> **High Impact + High Uncertainty**

These are the assumptions most worth validating first.

This is consistent with assumption-mapping practice, where teams identify critical hypotheses and prioritize those with limited supporting evidence.

## 5. Highest-Risk Assumptions

Based on the current understanding of the business, the following deserve particular attention:

### Customer Demand

> Drivers have a sufficiently significant parking problem that they are willing to change their current behavior.

### Pre-Booking

> Drivers see enough value in securing parking in advance to use a reservation-based service.

### Owner Willingness

> Owners are willing to make unused parking capacity available to people outside their existing network.

### Marketplace Liquidity

> A target location can obtain enough relevant parking supply and customer demand for the marketplace to be useful.

### Willingness to Pay

> Customers will accept the total price, including applicable Peter Park Here fees.

### Owner Economics

> The income generated from listing parking is sufficient to motivate owners to participate.

### Trust

> Both customers and owners can develop sufficient trust to transact through the marketplace.

These assumptions are closely connected. If customers do not want the service, owner supply does not create a viable marketplace. If owners do not supply parking, customer demand cannot be satisfied.

---

## 6. Validation Methods

For a real startup, these assumptions could be tested using lightweight experiments such as:

- Customer interviews
- Parking-owner interviews
- Clickable prototypes
- Landing pages
- Listing experiments
- Pricing experiments
- Small geographic pilots
- Actual booking/payment experiments

The goal is not to prove every assumption immediately.

The goal is to test the **riskiest assumptions first** with the smallest experiment capable of producing useful evidence.

Lean Startup describes this as a Build–Measure–Learn process: build only enough to generate learning, measure the response, and use that learning to determine the next action.

---

## 7. Build–Measure–Learn

The basic learning loop is:

```text
Identify assumption
        ↓
Prioritize risk
        ↓
Design smallest useful test
        ↓
Build / run experiment
        ↓
Measure evidence
        ↓
Learn
        ↓
Keep / modify / reject assumption
        ↓
Repeat
```

A successful experiment is not necessarily one that confirms the original idea.

It is one that produces useful evidence for making the next decision.

---

## 8. Learning-Project Approach

Peter Park Here is also a learning project, so we do not need to perform every real-world validation exercise.

Where actual validation is not practical, we will:

1. Document the assumption.
2. Explain why it matters.
3. Identify how it could be validated in a real startup.
4. Mark it as **unvalidated**.
5. Avoid presenting it as a fact.

This allows us to learn hypothesis-driven product development without spending weeks performing formal market research.

---

## 9. What We Should Not Do Yet

We should **not**:

- Treat assumptions as confirmed requirements.
- Build features simply because they appear in an assumption.
- Spend weeks validating every minor assumption.
- Create a detailed market study before identifying the riskiest hypotheses.
- Treat competitor features as proof that our customers will want the same features.

The purpose is to identify the assumptions that matter most and understand how they could be tested.

---

## 10. Exit Criteria

Step 6 is complete when:

> **We know which assumptions are important, which are uncertain, how they could be validated, and which assumptions must remain explicitly classified as hypotheses.**

At this point, we have progressed from:

**Problem → Users → Value → Business Model → Assumptions**

The next step can therefore begin translating these learnings into **Business Goals and Business Requirements**.
